# Section Guidance

Read the part relevant to the requested section and apply the [launch-presentation principles](argument-and-narrative.md). Section names and ordering vary by discipline and article type; preserve the author's appropriate conventions.

## Title and keywords

Name the actual research object and distinguishing contribution at a level the intended audience can understand. Avoid an acronym stack, unverified priority claims, or a title broader than the evidence. A descriptive title can be more accurate than a claim title for exploratory work. Use a subtitle only if it adds useful information or the venue expects it.

Choose searchable keywords from the topic, method, and application. Verify any controlled vocabulary against the target venue when required.

## Abstract

Make the abstract intelligible as a standalone account of the study. A common progression is problem and gap → approach or insight → principal evidence → supported implication. Follow structured headings when the article type requires them.

State what was studied and how, rather than promising what the paper will discuss. Give the result that most directly answers the research question. For quantitative findings, retain the comparison condition, relevant metric, and magnitude. Include uncertainty or sample context when omitting it would mislead.

Do not overload the opening with hardware names, abbreviations, dataset identifiers, or every method component. Define indispensable abbreviations and omit the rest. Do not add references, equations, or section pointers unless appropriate for the venue. Check the actual abstract word limit rather than assuming a universal length.

When abstract and body disagree, resolve the discrepancy from the evidence. Never polish an unsupported abstract claim into a more confident one.

## Introduction

Establish the research object and concrete need. Explain what current approaches or knowledge already achieve, then isolate the unresolved issue that motivates this study. Connect the issue to the central insight and explain how the contribution addresses it.

Use citations to substantiate the characterization of prior work. “Few studies exist” is not a sufficient research gap; specify the missing condition, explanatory account, capability, population, or evidence. Distinguish an absence of studies from limitations in studies that do exist.

Preview enough of the method for readers to understand the idea without reproducing the methods section. State the principal result and contribution at the level supported by the evaluation. A contribution list is useful when it clarifies distinct increments, not when it repeats the same claim in different wording.

If the work extends a prior version, describe what the earlier version established and what this manuscript adds. Verify disclosure and anonymous-citation rules for the actual venue.

## Background and motivation

Introduce only the concepts needed to understand the question and design. Define notation before using it and separate established facts from the paper's own observations.

A motivating experiment or example should expose a specific obstacle. Give enough context to interpret it and identify synthetic or illustrative material. Do not present an illustrative case as a representative empirical result.

Explain coupled constraints together when their interaction motivates the design. Do not fragment one central problem into unrelated optimization opportunities merely to create more subsections.

## Related work or literature review

Group prior work by research question, mechanism, assumption, evidence, or unresolved issue. Synthesize what the group establishes and explain the relationship to the present study. A sequence of author-by-author summaries rarely establishes a gap on its own.

Compare like with like. Describe differences in aims, settings, precision, data, resources, and evidence before claiming superiority. Credit the closest approaches accurately and include conflicting evidence where relevant.

Verify every substantive characterization against the source. A title, abstract, or citation count alone does not establish a paper's complete method or limitations. State when access is insufficient to verify a claim.

## Methods, materials, and data

Begin with the study design or method overview. Explain inputs, outputs, assumptions, components, and dependencies before implementation detail. Connect design choices to the requirements they satisfy and the costs they introduce.

For empirical work, describe sampling or selection, inclusion and exclusion criteria, collection procedures, measurement definitions, preprocessing, and analysis choices as relevant. Separate choices specified before analysis from exploratory choices when that distinction matters.

For algorithms and systems, define the data representation, operations, invariants, scheduling or synchronization conditions, complexity, and resource requirements needed to understand the claims. A diagram and pseudocode should agree with the actual implementation.

For theoretical work, make definitions, assumptions, quantifiers, theorem statements, and proof dependencies explicit. Separate examples and intuition from proof. Check that a claimed generalization does not require stronger assumptions than the theorem states.

For qualitative work, describe the setting, researcher position where relevant, recruitment or material selection, analytic approach, coding or interpretive procedure, and basis for the claims. Do not force quantitative validity terminology onto a different epistemic framework.

Report enough detail to evaluate and reproduce the procedure where applicable. Do not describe an unavailable procedure as reproducible solely because the prose is detailed.

## Experimental setup or analysis protocol

Define the study questions, comparison conditions, variables, metrics, and aggregation before presenting outcomes. Explain what each dataset, workload, participant group, or test case contributes to the question.

Identify reference methods and versions, configuration differences, tuning procedures, resource budgets, and criteria for accepting or excluding observations. Explain material departures from the protocol.

Use [Evidence and methods](evidence-and-methods.md) for reporting controls, uncertainty, timing boundaries, and failed measurements. Detailed operational records may belong in supporting material while the main text retains information necessary to interpret the claims.

## Results or evaluation

Order evidence around the questions rather than the order of experiment execution. Depending on the argument, present the main effect before decomposition, mechanism analysis, sensitivity, cost, and validity checks. A different order is appropriate when a proof or prerequisite result must come first.

Lead each subsection with its purpose or principal finding. Select informative values and comparisons; avoid reading tables aloud. Explain trends with supported reasoning and distinguish exploratory interpretations from direct observations.

Explain less favorable comparisons through the method's objective and supported trade-offs. Keep full failure and measurement records as research evidence; do not turn the results section into a list of weaknesses. Connect local or component results to the study's overall objective without changing the measurement scope.

## Discussion

Explain how the findings answer the question and relate to prior knowledge. Distinguish direct findings from interpretation and practical implications. Present alternatives through their objectives and trade-offs.

State applicable conditions positively in the setup or discussion, without repeating them after every finding. Do not add an unsolicited limitations section. If the author requests one or the verified venue rules require one, keep it specific and place it where requested.

Describe portability or transfer as a set of requirements and evidence. An untested extension is a proposal, not an established capability. Do not infer energy savings, safety, fairness, clinical utility, or policy benefit from a performance metric alone.

## Conclusion

Restate the question answered, the central contribution, and the strongest supported finding. Use the terminology and scope already established. Avoid copying the abstract verbatim or listing every implementation component.

Reinforce the memorable contribution without a self-negating closing paragraph. A future direction should follow from the study and be stated as future work. Do not introduce new evidence or promise that the approach resolves problems the manuscript did not examine.

## Supplementary material

Use supplementary material for detail that supports inspection without obscuring the central argument, subject to actual venue rules. Keep essential definitions and comparison conditions at the point needed to understand the argument.

Ensure labels, versions, cross-references, and anonymity agree with the manuscript. Do not assume appendices are excluded from a page limit or that reviewers must consult a repository.
