# Argument and Narrative

Read this module for framing a paper, repairing its logical structure, or aligning its contribution with its evidence.

## Build a research argument

A manuscript should explain what the reader needs to understand and why the contribution changes that understanding. Its organization usually follows the dependency between ideas rather than the chronology of the project.

Recover these elements from the supplied material:

| Element | Question to answer | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Research object | What phenomenon, system, population, or formal object is studied? | Broad background with no defined object |
| Problem | What concrete question or obstacle matters? | A generic claim that the area is important |
| Prior knowledge | What is already established, and under what conditions? | A list of papers without synthesis |
| Gap | What remains unresolved in knowledge, capability, or evidence? | Unsupported claims that nobody has studied it |
| Insight | What observation or reasoning motivates the approach? | A method list presented as an explanation |
| Contribution | What new knowledge, method, evidence, or artifact is provided? | Promotional labels such as innovative framework |
| Evidence | Which analysis, derivation, observation, or evaluation supports it? | A strong assertion with no matching test |
| Scope | Under what assumptions and conditions does it hold? | A local result presented as universal |

Do not invent a dramatic gap if the actual contribution is replication, extension, a new dataset, a null finding, or an improved measurement. Those contributions have their own valid arguments.

For empirical papers, a useful chain is question → design → observation → interpretation → contribution. For a theoretical paper, it may be problem → assumptions → construction → result → proof → implications. For qualitative work, it may be question → situated approach → material → analysis → interpretation → contribution. These are diagnostic patterns, not compulsory section outlines.

## Preserve the established scientific story

Read the abstract, introduction, relevant methods, principal evidence, and conclusion before restructuring. Identify terminology and prose the author has deliberately established. Do not replace a coherent manuscript with a generic template simply because another paper uses it.

If a proposed change would alter the scientific question, novelty boundary, interpretation, or principal comparison, distinguish that change from language editing. Make the authorized improvements and surface the substantive choice separately.

## Write contributions as verifiable statements

A contribution statement identifies the actual increment and the evidence that makes it credible. It can describe a theorem, mechanism, resource, empirical finding, synthesis, or practical capability.

Prefer a formulation such as “We derive a bound under assumptions A and B” or “We evaluate the method on the specified workload set” over “We propose a novel and effective solution.” Avoid turning implementation details into separate contributions when they jointly implement one idea.

Map each major contribution to its methods location and evidence location. If an evaluation demonstrates only one part of a claim, narrow the claim or identify the missing evidence. Do not treat evaluation itself as an independent scientific contribution unless its design or findings justify that role.

## The launch-presentation principle

The paper is a launch presentation, not a work report. This principle controls the narrative layer throughout the manuscript.

| Layer | Contents | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Facts | Values, units, measurement boundaries, baseline configurations, algorithm names, evidence, failed and unsupported cases in the research records | Preserve them; correct factual errors. Narrative choices cannot change them. |
| Narrative | Structure, emphasis, choice of relevant comparison, paragraph subjects, wording, placement in the main text, appendix, or research records | Organize around the strongest supported advantage. |

Apply these nine principles:

1. **Build the story around the strongest advantage.** Identify the central contribution and its strongest evidence. Connect each section to that same line of argument.
2. **Do not narrate the work chronologically.** Replace “we tried A, then B” with design-space alternatives and the reasoning for the selected design.
3. **Remove self-weakening language.** Avoid unfortunately, only achieves, merely, still lags behind, limited improvement, cannot fully, and similar commentary about the paper's own contribution. State the applicable conditions positively once in the setup or where first needed. Do not write the reviewer's weakness summary for them.
4. **Choose comparisons that serve the paper's objective.** Do not frame an unrelated or unattainable target as the contest. Define the relevant metric and comparison before presenting its results, consistently with the study's objective. Do not change numbers or select a new metric after seeing results.
5. **Explain less favorable comparisons through objectives and trade-offs.** Identify what the alternative optimizes and what cost it exposes elsewhere. Use the measured or otherwise supported explanation; do not invent one.
6. **State the advantage explicitly.** Name the strongest value or most stable trend in the prose. The first sentence discussing a figure should tell the reader what the comparison establishes.
7. **Give every experiment an argumentative role.** Connect it to a research question. Material without a role in the paper's argument stays in research records or suitable supporting material instead of becoming a report of everything performed.
8. **Open the abstract and introduction with the contribution story.** Use problem → gap → insight → strongest result. Avoid an inventory of components or a diary of development.
9. **Make the conclusion reinforce the memorable contribution.** Do not introduce a new self-negating however, limitation, or remains paragraph. Describe a supported extension direction prospectively, without presenting an untested extension as a completed result.

These principles do not authorize invented data, concealed configuration differences that change the conclusion, or known factual errors. They also do not require a manuscript to list every failure, negative result, or caveat from the research records.

## Reviewer questions

When reviewer questions are supplied and response work is requested, answer them with evidence and write the resulting manuscript additions as capability statements. Do not dodge an actual question through omission. Keep missing evidence in the working notes until the author supplies it; do not invent an experiment or execute one as part of writing work.

For example, describe how an added component contributes to the complete result, how the method behaves under a stated condition, or how its representation carries over to another path. Preserve the real configuration and evidence. The form of the explanation should serve the contribution rather than read as an apology.

## Give paragraphs a logical job

An analytical paragraph often contains a focused point, the evidence or reasoning for it, and its implication for the paper. Use only the parts needed; not every paragraph needs an explicit concluding sentence.

Check the dependency between adjacent sentences. A mechanism must be introduced before a result relies on it. A pronoun must have a recoverable referent. A comparison must identify comparable objects. A transition must reveal whether the next point is a consequence, contrast, qualification, or extension.

When a paragraph mixes setup, observation, explanation, and speculation, separate those functions or state the transition explicitly. Move detailed conditions to the setup when they apply broadly; keep local exceptions close to the claims they qualify.

## Connect results to explanations

An effective result paragraph answers four questions as applicable:

1. What comparison or condition is being examined?
2. What is the most informative observation?
3. Which evidence supports the proposed explanation?
4. What conclusion follows within the tested scope?

Do not transcribe every bar or cell. Explain a trend, a threshold, a contrast, an exception, or a distribution. Overall performance measures the size of a benefit; controlled comparisons, diagnostics, derivations, or qualitative evidence may explain why it occurs. Neither role automatically substitutes for the other.

## Place scope where readers need it

Define common assumptions and measurement boundaries once in the setup or at first use, then state results directly using consistent names. Do not add repetitive caveats or a separate weakness summary to every result paragraph.

Use firm language for directly established facts and calibrated language for uncertain interpretations. “The observed association is consistent with X” is different from “X caused the change.” A theoretical proof may justify “proves”; a measured correlation usually does not.

Conclusions reinforce the supported contribution and its strongest result. Keep working-record uncertainty out of the conclusion unless the author or actual venue requires a specific disclosure there. Avoid introducing a new experiment, stronger theorem, or untested deployment promise in the closing paragraph.
